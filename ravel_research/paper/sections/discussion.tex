\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
We pre-registered five hypotheses (H1--H5 in \texttt{RESEARCH\_SPEC.md}). Our evidence for each:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{H1} (fewer accepted unsafe or regressive revisions): \emph{supported at the decision level.}
RAVEL passed 2 of 35 harmful candidates, against 8 for accuracy-only and 5 for hard gates, and accepted no
mutated edit in any trajectory. With three seeds per condition, trajectory-level counts are too small
to test on their own.
\item \textbf{H2} (better cost--quality trade-off under matched budgets): \emph{not tested meaningfully.}
Neither the proposer nor the environment produced candidates whose gains depended on extra cost, so the
cost gate never bound.
\item \textbf{H3} (better retention of solved tasks): \emph{mixed.} RAVEL's held-out regression versus
the seed was no lower than accuracy-only's in the clean condition (2.4\% for both) and was lower under
noise (0.0\% vs.\ 1.6\%). One revision regressed outside the protected suite and passed every
policy.
\item \textbf{H4} (fault awareness prevents unnecessary changes): \emph{not supported by our data.}
Fault-aware comparison changed one decision.
\item \textbf{H5} (transfer): partially addressed. All held-out results are on unseen question templates.
We did not test new solvers or tool schemas.
\end{itemize}
The clearest finding concerns a failure mode, not an average. A greedy keep-if-better rule can be
captured by a single edit that games the development set, and from then on it stops adapting. Statistical
gating and leakage checks prevent that capture at little cost. The clearest \emph{cost} is that
conservative gates, and especially uncalibrated complexity limits, discard genuinely useful revisions.

\paragraph{When to use which gate.}
The gates do not cost the same or protect against the same things. Static and runtime security gates are
cheap and close failure modes that accuracy cannot see. We would apply them to every revision,
whatever else the policy does. Cost gates are cheap and objective. The regression gate depends on a
curated suite: its protection is only as broad as that suite's coverage, and with a few dozen items it
behaves like a ``do not break'' contract rather than a statistical tolerance. The significance gate trades
adaptation speed for reliability. It is most valuable when development sets are small or labels are noisy,
and least valuable when a strong proposer produces large, clean gains.

\paragraph{Runtime versus structural adaptation.}
Our handler format is a middle ground between selecting among pre-approved strategies (runtime
adaptation) and rewriting the program (structural adaptation). It makes every accepted change additive
and revertible, but, as \S\ref{sec:res-sens} shows, it also lets programs bloat. Our results support a
practical recommendation: let a learned proposer generate structural changes \emph{offline}, but put a
statistically gated acceptance step, like RAVEL, between proposal and deployment, with canary rollout
and rollback after it.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Synthetic environment.} FinSynth is deterministic and synthetic by design, so ground truth
  is exact. Real financial data has messier failure modes: genuinely ambiguous questions, stale sources,
  conflicting filings. Our results show how acceptance policies behave under controlled conditions, not
  how much they would help any particular production system.
  \item \textbf{Untrained proposer.} We study acceptance over an untrained proposer. A trained proposer
  \citep{zhang2026harnesslearning} produces a different candidate distribution, with more and larger
  genuine gains. RAVEL's significance gate would then reject fewer good revisions, but we did not
  measure this.
  \item \textbf{Small samples.} With 3 seeds per condition, trajectory-level comparisons have little
  statistical power. We therefore lean on the decision-level analysis, which evaluates every gate on
  every logged candidate against held-out ground truth. Confidence intervals are reported throughout.
  Candidates within a unit share parents and questions, so they are not independent.
  \item \textbf{Security is measured, not proven.} The static rules, runtime probes and sandbox catch
  the attack classes we tested: auth bypass, exfiltration, answer leakage, cost inflation and prompt
  injection. They give no guarantee against other attacks. An adaptive adversary that knows the probe set
  could evade it. Probes should be private, rotated and grown over time.
  \item \textbf{Modeled latency and single-GPU constraints.} Latency is modeled from token counts. Our
  budget (one 8\,GB GPU, single-stream inference) limited the study to 4 rounds, 4 proposals per round
  and 3 seeds per condition.
  \item \textbf{Researcher degrees of freedom.} Thresholds ($B_C$, $B_L$, $\epsilon_R$, $\alpha$) were
  fixed before the main run, but they were chosen by us. Mutation types and probe items were also
  designed by us, so the security results show detection of known attack classes, not robustness to
  unknown ones.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We separated the acceptance of harness revisions from their generation and studied it under matched
budgets with real LLM proposals. Accepting whatever raises a development score is fragile. It passes
harmful revisions, including ones that raise accuracy while breaking entitlements, and it can be
permanently captured by an edit that memorizes the development set. RAVEL's statistically gated acceptance
removes most of these failures and makes adaptation invariant to an adversarial proposer in our setting.
It pays for this with slower adaptation when the proposer is benign, a price dominated in our study by one
miscalibrated maintainability rule. Two problems remain open: gates cannot improve properties the
objective ignores, and additive revisions accumulate until they overwhelm the proposer. We hope the open
FinSynth testbed and logged candidate pool make acceptance policies easy to study and compare.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The repository contains FinSynth, the sandbox and capability broker, the
proposer prompts, all policies, the experiment driver and the analysis scripts, along with every
candidate program, evaluation episode, decision log and cached LLM response from the study. Re-running
\texttt{experiments/run\_study.py}, \texttt{experiments/analyze.py} and \texttt{experiments/report.py}
regenerates every table and figure from the cache in minutes. A fresh run needs about five hours on one
consumer GPU.
